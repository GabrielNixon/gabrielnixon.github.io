## Rarest of the Rare: What Makes a Crime Beyond Forgiveness?

What makes a crime so brutal that even life in prison feels too little?  
What crosses the line where reform is no longer seen as an option?  
In India, we call this kind of punishment the **"rarest of the rare"** — a doctrine not clearly defined, yet powerful enough to decide life or death.

---

## Where It All Began

The idea started gaining legal shape in the 1980s.  
While **Nathuram Godse** was the first to be hanged post-Independence (for assassinating Gandhi), the first proper case under this doctrine was:

### **Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980)**

- Bachan Singh had already served time for killing his wife.
- After his release, he lived with his cousin.
- On July 4, 1977, he **axed three of his cousin's children** after an argument.

The trial court sentenced him to death. He appealed to the Supreme Court, questioning whether the death penalty itself violated **Article 21** (*Right to Life*).

The court upheld the sentence and introduced the **"rarest of the rare"** doctrine.  
It wasn’t strictly defined, but meant that *only in exceptional cases where life imprisonment isn't enough*, the death penalty can be applied.

---

## Rajendra Prasad: The Turning Point

### **Rajendra Prasad v. State of Uttar Pradesh**

- He had killed someone with an axe over a land dispute.
- He was given the death penalty, but **Justice Krishna Iyer** overturned the sentence.

This case introduced:
- **Reformative justice**
- Idea that capital punishment must be **extremely rare**
- Focus on the **individual**, not just the act

Even if the crime is heinous, if the criminal is **capable of reform**, the death penalty may not be justified.

However, this logic didn’t help Bachan Singh — his death sentence was upheld.  
Notably, there’s no public record confirming whether he was actually executed.

---

## Executions in Independent India

Despite hundreds of death sentences, **executions are rare**.

### Executions since 1980 include:

- **Ranga and Billa (1982)** — Murdered Sanjay and Geeta Chopra.
- **Dhananjoy Chatterjee (2004)** — Rape and murder of Hetal Parekh.
- **Ajmal Kasab (2012)** — 26/11 Mumbai terrorist.
- **Afzal Guru (2013)** — 2001 Parliament attack.
- **Yakub Memon (2015)** — 1993 Bombay blasts.
- **Nirbhaya Convicts (2020)** — Gang-rape and murder in Delhi.

## Death Row Today

- **2023**: 120 new death sentences
- **2024**: 564 prisoners currently on death row  
  → *Highest in two decades*

Most of these will likely be **commuted to life imprisonment** during appeal.

---

## The First Woman on Death Row?

### **Shabnam Ali (2008)**

- A postgraduate from Amroha, UP
- Along with her partner **Salim**, sedated and axed **7 family members** — including a 10-month-old baby
- Supreme Court upheld the death penalty
- As of 2021: Awaiting execution at Mathura Jail

If executed, she would be the **first woman** hanged in independent India.

## So, What Is "Rarest of the Rare"?

There is **no fixed rule**.

Courts consider:
- Brutality of the act
- Motive and mental state
- Possibility of reform
- Social impact

It’s a blend of facts, context, and **judicial judgment**.

And yet, **actual executions are rare** — even when the law says death.

---

## The Unspoken Bias?

There are many equally brutal crimes — involving **MLAs**, wealthy families, or systemic scandals like **Ajmer** — that **don’t end in execution**.

Why?

Two-thirds of those on death row are **poor**.

That might seem like **class bias** — but:

- Most of India **is poor**
- Poor communities face more **state violence**
- They lack **legal defense**, education, and influence

So is the doctrine a **fair moral compass**, or just **an abstract phrase** used more harshly against those who **can’t fight back**?

That’s something worth asking.
