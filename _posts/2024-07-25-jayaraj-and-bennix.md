## Jayaraj and Bennix: When Power Becomes Violence

When we think about police brutality, the first case that often comes to mind is that of George Floyd. George Perry Floyd Jr. was murdered on May 25, 2020, by Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin, who knelt on his neck for over nine minutes during an arrest over a suspected counterfeit note. Floyd's dying words, "I can't breathe," sparked global protests and became a rallying cry against systemic racism and unchecked police power.

This led me to a harder question: why don’t we hear such stories from India, even when we know they happen?

Just seven days after Floyd’s death, something horrifying happened in Tamil Nadu — the custodial deaths of Jayaraj and his son Bennix. Unlike Floyd, their story barely made it past a few news cycles, even though it exposed one of the most brutal examples of state violence in recent memory.

During the COVID-19 lockdown, Tamil Nadu enforced a curfew that required shops to close by 8 PM. On one such night, Jayaraj was late by about 15 minutes. He was warned by the police and closed up. The next day, he reportedly made a comment to the officers — what exactly was said isn’t clear. But what happened next escalated into something monstrous.

Jayraj was arrested and taken into custody. Bennix, his son, came to the station to inquire and was also detained. Both were stripped, beaten continuously with batons, and subjected to extreme torture. Their skin tore. Muscle was exposed. They were reportedly sexually assaulted with iron rods. Their chest hair was pulled out. They bled through their clothes, which were sent home multiple times, soaked in blood, as police asked for replacements.

Under Indian law, a magistrate is required to approve custody or hospitalization—but the police bypassed this by conducting the process in a part of the station without CCTV. Eventually, the two were transferred 100 kilometers away. When they arrived at the new jail, their condition was so bad that they were sent directly to the hospital—where both were declared dead.

The deaths of Jayaraj and Bennix triggered brief national outrage. Videos and eyewitness accounts supported claims of brutal torture. The five police officers involved were arrested and charged with murder. The Madras High Court took suo moto cognizance, and the case was handed to the CBI. Bail was denied. The Tamil Nadu government announced reforms, including mandatory CCTV in all police stations.

But as of 2024, the trial is still pending.

The momentum faded. Structural reform stalled. News coverage disappeared. And like so many others, this case risked becoming another forgotten injustice.

## The Silence Is the Pattern

Why didn’t Jayaraj and Bennix receive the same attention George Floyd did? A large part of it is how Indian media handles police brutality. Stories involving abuse of power rarely reach national headlines unless they trigger immediate outrage. Even when they do, the coverage often dies down in days. There is no consistent pressure, no follow-up, and no sustained public demand for change.

In India, custodial deaths are often normalized or invisibilized. They are quietly filed under local crime briefs, rarely investigated by independent bodies, and even more rarely prosecuted to the end. When the victims are poor, lower caste, or powerless, the silence is deeper.

This is not an isolated case.

In Manipur (2016), Thangjam Manorama, a 32-year-old woman, was arrested by the Assam Rifles and later found dead with signs of sexual violence. The outrage led to protests but no convictions.

In Uttar Pradesh (2018), Pushpendra Yadav, a journalist, was killed in what the police called an “encounter.” Eyewitnesses say he was shot without provocation. Again, no accountability.

India sees dozens of custodial deaths every year, but few even make headlines. The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) releases yearly figures, but these rely heavily on self-reporting by police departments. There is no central, independent, public-facing database tracking custodial torture or deaths. This lack of transparency makes systemic reform almost impossible.

## Final Thought

So where do we draw the line between power and rules? When those meant to protect justice have unchecked authority to break it, what guarantees accountability—and what defines impunity?

Who gets protected by the law, and who gets punished by it?

Are silence and delay just forms of complicity?

We ask why no one speaks up—but what happens when no one listens?
